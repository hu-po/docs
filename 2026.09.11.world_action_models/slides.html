A model of the world

Before it predicts anything useful, a world model is a compressed internal picture that gets run forward.

Ha and Schmidhuber open the 2018 World Models paper with a panel from Scott McCloud's Understanding Comics. Their framing is that an agent never acts on the world directly, only on a compressed internal model of it assembled from limited sensory input. What gets thrown away in that compression is the subject of everything that follows.

Ha et al. · World Models · Fig. 1 · arXiv:1803.10122

The original architecture splits three ways: a Vision module (V) compresses each frame into a latent code, a Memory module (M), an RNN, predicts the next latent given the action taken, and a small Controller (C) maps those to actions. The controller is deliberately tiny, so nearly all the capacity sits in the parts that model the world rather than the part that acts. Perception, dynamics, policy is still the skeleton of most world models today.

Ha et al. · World Models · Fig. 4 · arXiv:1803.10122

This policy was trained entirely inside the RNN's hallucinated version of VizDoom, never touching the real environment during learning, and is then deployed back into actual VizDoom. It is the result that named the field: if the learned model is good enough, an agent can practise in imagination and the skill transfers. It also introduces the failure mode that recurs throughout the deck, since an agent free to practise in its own dream will happily exploit that dream's flaws.

Ha et al. · World Models · Fig. 16 · arXiv:1803.10122

A timeline of world models for robotic policy learning, split into two branches. The upper branch tracks world model as policy, moving from early decoupled video-generation-plus-inverse-dynamics pipelines toward tighter single-backbone, MoE/MoT and latent designs. The lower branch tracks world model as simulator, from rollout-based validation and candidate evaluation toward full learned environments. Nearly every paper in this deck sits on one of these two branches.

Hou et al. · World Models for Robot Learning: A Survey · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2605.00080

The minimal definition. A world model takes the current observation and an action and predicts the next observation, and that is all it does. Nothing here emits an action, so it has to be paired with a planner or a policy to be useful for control. The predicted observation can live in any space the designer picks: raw pixels, latents, or point clouds.

Zhang et al. · From World Models to World Action Models · Fig. 4 (left) · arXiv:2607.00836

The world action model differs in exactly one place: the action is now an output rather than an input. The predicted next observation is still there but sits in parentheses, meaning it is optional at inference time. That parenthesis is the whole argument of the fourth section of this deck, and several later papers exist mainly to discard the video branch and keep only the action.

Zhang et al. · From World Models to World Action Models · Fig. 4 (right) · arXiv:2607.00836

A two-axis map of observation-space world models. The vertical axis is how spatially explicit the observation is, from RGB up through multi-view RGB, RGB-D and point clouds; the horizontal axis is how abstract the action conditioning is, from low-level robot actions through interface actions and latent actions to plain language. Models are plotted into that space, with Genie3, Cosmos predict2, UniPi and SuSIE toward the language end and ParticleFormer and Point World at the point-cloud extreme. The authors frame these as trade-offs between spatial understanding, data availability and downstream usability rather than a ranking.

Zhang et al. · From World Models to World Action Models · Fig. 6 · arXiv:2607.00836

Four ways to wire future prediction into a policy. Imagine-then-execute generates future frames and then recovers actions with inverse dynamics; video-feature-conditioned models skip the pixels and condition on features; joint video-action modelling emits both at once; and auxiliary video prediction uses the video branch only during training. Roughly twenty named models hang off these four boxes, including LingBot-VA and CosmosPolicy, which post the winning numbers in the robustness study later in the deck.

Zhang et al. · From World Models to World Action Models · Fig. 8 · arXiv:2607.00836

Three architectures side by side. Panel (a) is a VLA, mapping observation and instruction straight to action; panel (b) is a world action model, which adds a future-prediction branch; panel (c) is WLA, using an autoregressive transformer to predict the next state from two complementary representations at once, high-level textual intention and low-level physical dynamics. Panel (d) reports that WLA-0 reaches strong results with only 2B active parameters and no embodied pretraining.

Yang et al. · WLA · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.05979

A completely different reading of the phrase world model. Here an LLM is asked to write a symbolic PDDL domain from a natural-language description, producing explicit predicates, actions, preconditions and effects rather than pixels. The generated domain is passed through a syntax parser, executed, and scored against a reference on structural similarity and component-wise F1. It is a useful counterweight before the rest of the deck commits to video prediction as the default representation.

Hu et al. · Text2World · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2502.13092

Learning to dream

The RL lineage: train a simulator from experience, then do the learning inside it.

DreamerV3 across five very different benchmark families: Control Suite, Atari, ProcGen, DMLab and Minecraft. The headline is that a single fixed set of hyperparameters beats expert algorithms tuned individually per domain, which is what made world-model RL look like a general recipe rather than a per-task craft. Panel (b) is the Minecraft diamond result, obtained from scratch under sparse rewards, a problem that previously required human demonstrations or hand-written heuristics.

Hafner et al. · DreamerV3 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2301.04104

DayDreamer runs the Dreamer loop directly on hardware with no simulator anywhere. The current policy collects real experience, that experience goes into a replay buffer, the world model is trained on replayed sequences by supervised learning, and an actor-critic optimises the policy purely on imagined rollouts inside the model's latent space. Data collection and learning run in parallel, so the robot keeps moving while the networks update. This is the bridge between the 2018 dream-transfer result and physical robots.

Wu et al. · DayDreamer · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2206.14176

The same quadruped after its walking behaviour is deliberately perturbed. Within ten minutes of real-world learning it adapts to withstand pushes and to roll back onto its feet when flipped. Ten minutes is the number worth holding onto: the appeal of learning inside a world model is sample efficiency, and this is what that buys on hardware.

Wu et al. · DayDreamer · Fig. 8 · arXiv:2206.14176

TD-MPC2 encodes observations into a normalised latent and then recurrently predicts actions, rewards and terminal values, without ever decoding future observations back into pixels. That omission is the point. Everything else in this section reconstructs what the future looks like, whereas TD-MPC2 argues you only need to predict the quantities that planning actually consumes. One architecture with fixed hyperparameters covers 104 tasks.

Hansen et al. · TD-MPC2 · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2310.16828

Three different starting frames from Genie's robotics dataset, each column showing the result of applying the same latent action five times in a row. The learned actions turn out to mean something consistent across scenes, corresponding to down, up and left. Genie was trained with no action labels at all, so this consistency is emergent rather than supervised, which is the property that makes action-free internet video usable as robot pretraining data.

Bruce et al. · Genie · Fig. 12 · arXiv:2402.15391

Diffusion Forcing sits between two existing regimes. Next-token prediction trains causally on ground-truth sequences with teacher forcing; full-sequence diffusion denoises every frame at once at a shared noise level with a non-causal architecture. Diffusion Forcing instead gives each frame in the sequence its own independent noise level while keeping the architecture causal. That combination lets one model roll out variable-length sequences and stay stable far past its training horizon.

Chen et al. · Diffusion Forcing · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2407.01392

DreamDojo learns physical knowledge from 44k hours of egocentric human video, using continuous latent actions as unified labels to work around the absence of action annotations. After post-training and distillation onto target robots it predicts the future in real time under continuous action control. The four panels track the pipeline from human video pretraining through robot post-training and autoregressive distillation to applications: policy evaluation without real-world deployment, live teleoperation, and online model-based planning.

Gao et al. · DreamDojo · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2602.06949

An ANYmal D quadruped and a Unitree G1 humanoid deployed from policies trained offline inside the world model, at three strengths of uncertainty penalty. With too little penalty the policy exploits places where the model is wrong and the robots collide or fail to walk at all. With too much, the policies go conservative and barely move. The middle setting is where the robot explores novel behaviour in imagination while staying inside the region the model actually knows.

Li et al. · Uncertainty-Aware Robotic World Model · Fig. 7 · arXiv:2504.16680

Three real robots solving tasks zero-shot from policies trained entirely from scratch inside learned world models: Push-T on a tabletop manipulator, egocentric grasp-and-lift on a G1 humanoid, and egocentric push-cube on a Go2 quadruped. No real-world interaction was used to train the policies themselves. This closes the loop Ha and Schmidhuber opened in 2018, now across three embodiments rather than one video game.

Amigo et al. · Coupled Local and Global World Models · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2602.06219

The policy-scaling era

What the field was betting on when the channel went quiet: bolt an action head onto a big pretrained model.

RT-2's move was to treat robot actions as just another language. Actions are cast into text tokens and trained jointly with internet-scale vision-language data, then de-tokenized back into commands at inference for closed-loop control. The payoff is that a vision-language model's generalization, semantic understanding and reasoning transfer into control for free. This is the idea the world-model camp is arguing against for the rest of the deck: mapping observation to action directly, with no model of how the world evolves.

Brohan et al. · RT-2 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2307.15818

OpenVLA is the open reference implementation of that idea, and its architecture is worth seeing concretely. An image and a language instruction go in and 7-dimensional robot control actions come out, via three parts: a vision encoder concatenating DINOv2 and SigLIP features, a projector mapping those into the language embedding space, and a Llama 2 7B backbone. Note the proportions, since almost all of the parameters are the language model.

Kim et al. · OpenVLA · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2406.09246

Diffusion Policy takes a different route to the same goal. At each step the policy consumes the last few observations and emits a chunk of future actions, and rather than predicting those actions directly it starts from Gaussian noise and denoises them over K iterations, with the observation conditioning injected via FiLM at every convolution layer. Treating the action chunk itself as the thing to denoise is what makes it good at multimodal behaviour, where several different actions are all valid.

Chi et al. · Diffusion Policy · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2303.04137

Pi-0.5 cleaning a kitchen that was not in its training data. It is given only general instructions such as close the cabinets, put the items in the drawer, wipe the spill, and it handles them by predicting its own subtasks (pick up the plate) and then emitting low-level actions for each. This is the capability bar the world-model camp has to clear, and it comes from data scale and a hierarchical policy rather than from any explicit model of physics.

Intelligence et al. · π₀.₅ · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2504.16054

GR00T N1's dual-system split, which recurs across many later models in this deck. Image observations and the language instruction become tokens for a vision-language model backbone; those outputs, plus robot state and action encodings, feed a diffusion transformer that emits motor actions. The division of labour is slow semantic reasoning in the VLM and fast continuous control in the diffusion head, because the two run at very different natural frequencies.

NVIDIA et al. · GR00T N1 · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2503.14734

GR00T N1's training corpus drawn as a pyramid, where quantity falls and embodiment-specificity rises as you move up. Web and human video sit at the wide base, synthetic and simulated data in the middle, and real teleoperation on the target robot at the narrow tip. This picture is the economic argument behind the whole video-pretraining thesis: the scarce, expensive data is exactly the data you need most, so anything that converts the base of the pyramid into usable supervision is worth a great deal.

NVIDIA et al. · GR00T N1 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2503.14734

The same task run on two embodiments in ATOM-Bench. The top row is a Franka Panda pouring coffee beans from a cup into a bowl and succeeding throughout, marked with green ticks. The bottom row is the paired dual-arm version on a Cobot Magic, picking the bowl up with the left hand and pouring with the right, which fails midway. Coordination between two arms is not a small increment on single-arm skill, and the gap shows up even for strong policies.

Wu et al. · ATOM-Bench · Fig. 6 · arXiv:2606.16826

Prediction as the policy

Generate what should happen next, then extract the actions that get you there.

UniPi's framing, and the origin of this whole section: a text-conditional video generator is already a universal policy. Because video is a shared representation across simulation, real robots and YouTube, you can train one generator on all of it and then apply it downstream. What that buys is combinatorial language generalization, long-horizon planning, and internet-scale knowledge, none of which come easily to a policy trained only on robot trajectories.

Du et al. · UniPi · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2302.00111

SuSIE narrows the idea from whole videos to single frames. A pretrained image-editing model is asked to imagine the next subgoal image given the language command, and a low-level goal-reaching policy executes whatever actions get the robot to that image. Alternating the two solves the task. The appeal is that the imagining component is entirely off-the-shelf and was never trained on robots.

Black et al. · SuSIE · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2310.10639

Three rollouts on the same task, put the yellow bell pepper in the ceramic bowl. RT-2-X and an oracle goal-conditioned behaviour-cloning baseline both fail to grasp the pepper, while SuSIE's generated subgoals steer the low-level controller into a successful grasp. The claim being made is specific: the subgoal images are not just guidance about what to do next, they measurably improve the quality of low-level skill execution.

Black et al. · SuSIE · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2310.10639

Genie Envisioner packages the three uses of a world model into one platform. GE-Base is the underlying video world model encoding the spatial, temporal and semantic structure of robot interaction; GE-Act builds on it to infer instruction-conditioned policies; GE-Sim reuses the same model as an action-conditioned simulator. That one artifact serves as pretraining, policy and simulator is a good summary of where this branch of the field has ended up.

Liao et al. · Genie Envisioner · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2508.05635

LingBot-VA 2.0 assembled end to end. A planner decomposes long-horizon goals into structured subtask context; a semantic visual-action tokenizer encodes both camera observations and human video prompts into visual latents; a mixture-of-experts video model and an action model then autoregressively predict future latents and robot actions together. Multi-chunk prediction adds supervision on several future chunks at once, and the autoregressive rollout feeds asynchronous execution so the robot runs in real time.

Zhang et al. · LingBot-VA 2.0 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2607.08639

Bridge-WA argues you do not need the whole future image, only the parts that bear on the action. It compresses the future into three compact representations: future tokens for the intended outcome, change maps for where the scene should change, and motion-flow maps for how that change should move. A lightweight predictor estimates them from current context and the action transformer conditions on them. The heavy future model and its offline cache are removed at deployment entirely.

Bai et al. · Bridge-WA · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2607.02195

The memory trade-off every world action model runs into. A sliding window is cheap but forgets long-range context; caching the full history preserves context but its cost grows linearly with trajectory length N. MemoryWAM proposes a hybrid: recent frames for short-term memory, anchor frames to mark event boundaries, and gist tokens compressing long-range history. That drops inference cost from O(N) toward sublinear while keeping the context the model actually needs.

Yang et al. · MemoryWAM · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.20562

The same pi-0.5 rollout run three ways across five tasks: in the real world, in Ctrl-World, and in Mem-World, which adds a wrist-view surfel memory. Reading across each row shows how far each model's imagined rollout drifts from what actually happened. The recurring failure in the memoryless version is objects that leave the wrist camera's view and are then forgotten or invented back incorrectly, which is precisely what a persistent geometric memory is there to fix.

Zheng et al. · Mem-World · Fig. 6 · arXiv:2606.18960

Where latent actions come from, in three stages. First, latent action quantization learns discrete actions between consecutive video frames with a VQ-VAE objective, fully unsupervised and with no action labels. Second, a VLM is pretrained to predict those latent actions from observations and instructions, which is behaviour cloning against invented labels. Third, a small set of genuinely action-labelled trajectories maps that latent space onto real end-effector deltas. This is the mechanism Genie, DreamDojo and MemoryWAM all rely on.

Ye et al. · LAPA · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2410.11758

One-step dynamics prediction compared against baselines, on scenes with several objects of different materials interacting at once. ParticleFormer works in 3D point-cloud space rather than pixels, which is the top-left corner of the design-space chart back in section one. The rightmost column is a block-wise attention heatmap showing which particles attend to which, exposing the interaction structure the model has learned both within and between materials.

Huang et al. · ParticleFormer · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2506.23126

Efficient-WAM attacks the cost side. Its claim is that you can capture the task-relevant object and robot dynamics without generating photorealistic video at all, since most of the pixels in a rendered future are irrelevant to the action. At 1B parameters it reports lower latency than prior world action models while holding task success in both simulation and the real world. Latency matters here because a model that denoises a future before every action chunk is competing with a reactive policy that simply acts.

Li et al. · Efficient-WAM · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.10040

NavWAM predicting future views during closed-loop navigation on a real robot. The distinction from earlier navigation world models is that they generated future views only to score candidate actions and then handed off to an external planner, whereas NavWAM represents future observations, goal-progress values and action chunks in one shared latent sequence. This is the same imagine-and-act pattern as the manipulation work in this section, applied to a mobile base.

Azuma et al. · NavWAM · Fig. 6 · arXiv:2606.13494

Does any of it generalize

Both camps claim robustness. These are the papers that perturb the benchmark and measure it.

Cosmos-Policy's predicted future frames against ground truth, in third-person and wrist views, under three perturbation types from LIBERO-Plus: sensor noise, lighting changes and background swaps. The top row is what actually happened, the bottom row is what the model predicted. The background column is the striking one, since the scene is replaced with a texture the model has never seen and the prediction still tracks the manipulation correctly.

Zhang et al. · Do World Action Models Generalize Better than VLAs? · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2603.22078

Three cases on RoboTwin 2.0-Plus, each comparing pi-0.5 against LingBot-VA with keyframes running left to right. In (a), beat block with hammer under sensor noise, pi-0.5 collides with the hammer and fails. In (b), handover block under a layout change, it collides with the red block on approach. Case (c) runs under heavy magenta lighting. These are the concrete failures behind the aggregate numbers, and they are collisions and mistimed approaches rather than the model misunderstanding the instruction.

Zhang et al. · Do World Action Models Generalize Better than VLAs? · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2603.22078

The seven perturbation axes of LIBERO-Plus laid out as columns: object layout, background textures, lighting, camera viewpoint, robot initial state, language instruction and sensor noise. The four rows are the LIBERO suites, spatial, object, goal and long-horizon. Each row is anchored on one base scene so all seven panels share identical objects and goals, which isolates the visual effect of each axis on its own. This is what benchmark robustness actually means in these papers.

Liu et al. · OA-WAM · Fig. 9 · arXiv:2605.06481

WorldArena 2.0 extends embodied world-model benchmarking along three axes at once: modality, adding visuotactile alongside vision; functionality, evaluating world models as RL environments rather than only as predictors; and platform, spanning RoboTwin 2.0, LIBERO and a real AgileX setup. The direction of travel is worth noting, since benchmarks are shifting from scoring how good the predicted video looks toward scoring what the prediction is useful for.

Shang et al. · WorldArena 2.0 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2605.17912

Success rate and task progress rate for LingBot-VA 2.0 against representative baselines, on real-world tasks rather than simulation. Task progress rate matters here alongside raw success, because a long-horizon policy that gets most of the way through a task is meaningfully different from one that fails at the first step, and binary success hides that entirely.

Zhang et al. · LingBot-VA 2.0 · Fig. 8 · arXiv:2607.08639

RoboWM-Bench's core idea, shown per task. The predicted video is on the left, and on the right that same prediction has been converted into robot actions and actually executed in simulation, with green ticks and red crosses marking whether the execution succeeded. It scores a world model on what its prediction does rather than on how plausible the video looks, which is a stricter and more relevant test than perceptual similarity.

Jiang et al. · RoboWM-Bench · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2604.19092

A comparison of world action model paradigms on both concept and numbers. Joint-WAM predicts future observations and serves as the efficiency reference; Fast-WAM removes test-time future imagination entirely and generalizes worse for it. LAWA keeps a latent action as the representation of future intention, matching Joint-WAM's performance at lower latency while beating Fast-WAM. It also gains more from action-free data than either, which is the argument that the imagination step is doing real work rather than burning compute.

Li et al. · LAWA · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2608.24882

Panel (a) contrasts a standard VLA trained only on action supervision with DriveVLA-W0, which is trained to predict future visual scenes as well as future actions. Panel (b) is the consequence: collision rate against training frames from 700K to 70M, where the world-modeling variant falls faster and ends 20.4% lower. The claim is not that world modeling adds a fixed increment, but that it changes the slope, because predicting scenes is a dense supervision signal where sparse action labels are not.

Li et al. · DriveVLA-W0 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2510.12796

The trajectory data is colour-coded three ways: success, recovery and failure. Prior VLAs use only the success trajectories, so when execution drifts off-distribution there is nothing in training that resembles the state the robot is now in. RePO-VLA assigns distinct supervision to all three kinds, slicing and resetting recovery trajectories and salvaging prefixes from failures. The right panel contrasts the outcomes: the baseline drifts and stops, while the recovery-trained policy corrects itself.

Liufu et al. · RePO-VLA · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2605.09410

RoboArena's answer to the evaluation problem. Rather than standardising environments and tasks, it aggregates crowd-sourced pairwise A/B comparisons across many labs, environments and tasks into a single global ranking, with evaluators free to choose their own scenes but required to run double-blind pairs. The instantiation spans seven academic institutions on the DROID platform, with over 600 pairwise real-robot episodes. This is the leaderboard the RoboWorld correlation figure in the next section is measured against.

Atreya et al. · RoboArena · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2506.18123

A dissenting result, and worth sitting with. RoboChallenge runs current VLAs on manually augmented and corrupted images, and reports that their outputs largely match despite the change, concluding these models are not sensitive to background change or occlusion. Almost everything else in this section treats perturbation robustness as the central difficulty. The two claims can be reconciled, since the benchmarks perturb simulated scenes far outside their training distribution while this tests modest corruptions on real hardware, but the tension is real and unresolved.

Yakefu et al. · RoboChallenge · Fig. 6 · arXiv:2510.17950

Prediction for other ends

If you can predict, you can also evaluate, monitor and steer — not just act.

RoboWorld evaluates a policy by rolling it out closed-loop inside a video world model, with no robot involved, and scoring the resulting rollout with a VLM judge that reasons about task progress rather than final state alone. The two halves both matter: the world model supplies the environment, and the judge supplies the reward signal that a simulator would normally give you for free. The resulting rankings correlate strongly with real-world evaluation.

Jeon et al. · RoboWorld · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2607.01060

Panel (a) is the headline, Pearson and Spearman correlation between RoboWorld's scores and the real RoboArena leaderboard, at r = 0.989. Panel (b) shows correlation improving as the number of evaluation trials grows, with diversity of environments and tasks mattering more than raw count. Panel (c) compares rollouts side by side in RoboWorld and in the real world for the same policies and tasks, sharing only the initial view, including a case where PaliGemma Fast fails in both for the same reason.

Jeon et al. · RoboWorld · Fig. 5 · arXiv:2607.01060

Long-horizon action-conditioned video prediction compared against prior models on manipulation tasks. The baselines degrade in specific, recognisable ways over a long rollout: inaccurate dynamics, robot pose drifting away from where it should be, artifacts accumulating frame over frame, and fine detail washing out. This system holds together for more than ten minutes of interaction at 15 FPS on a single RTX 4090, which is what makes it usable as an interactive environment rather than a demo.

Wang et al. · Interactive World Simulator · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2603.08546

Foresight in three stages. First an action-conditioned world model is fine-tuned on robot rollouts of images and actions. Second, at each timestep the model encodes the current observation into hidden latents and predicts action-conditioned future latents from the policy's proposed action chunk, and a failure score is trained on those latents using only task-level success or failure labels, with no per-frame annotation. Third, functional conformal prediction calibrates the alarm threshold. The detector is policy-agnostic.

Zhang et al. · Foresight · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.23085

A real ReactorX-200 arm running ACT on pick up banana and lion toy into basket, in an episode that succeeds. The failure score stays below its adaptive threshold throughout and every keyframe border stays green, so no alarm is raised. This is the control case that makes the next slide meaningful, since a detector that never fires on success is the harder half of the problem.

Zhang et al. · Foresight · Fig. 14 · arXiv:2606.23085

The same task and policy, in an episode that fails when the robot does not get the banana. The failure score crosses its threshold at inference step 6, marked by the dotted vertical line, and the keyframe borders flip from green to red at exactly that point. The alarm fires while the episode is still running rather than after the fact, which is the difference between failure detection and post-hoc scoring.

Zhang et al. · Foresight · Fig. 15 · arXiv:2606.23085

Left, the memory gap on occluded-object tasks: greedy reactive agents collapse to 0.6% success on the hardest chained-memory step, against 82.2% with this method. Right, where that comes from: a persistent SE(3) spatial anchor updated by forward kinematics so object locations survive leaving the camera view, a world-model-based MCTS tree that imagines future frames to plan over, and a hybrid geometric-semantic scorer that compensates for VLMs being poor at depth. Note this one is a two-author preprint whose figure is an AI-generated infographic, so it sits at a different tier from its neighbours.

Sidik et al. · 3D-ALP · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2604.11302

Driving scenarios generated from scratch by GAIA-2, across full surround-camera rigs and a wide span of conditions: dusk, snow, night, tunnels, wet roads. Nothing here is recorded footage. This is the same world-model idea as everything else in the deck, applied to autonomous driving, where the economics are different because collecting rare dangerous scenarios on real roads is not an option and generating them is.

Russell et al. · GAIA-2 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2503.20523

Two real-robot rollouts of the same task. The supervised baseline fails, placing the toy giraffe beside the target bowl rather than in it. The same policy finetuned with reinforcement learning against a learned reward model places it correctly inside. The reward model is what supplies the training signal here, which matters because reward is one of the four interfaces the position paper at the end of this deck names as missing.

Wu et al. · Large Reward Models · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2603.16065

A representative manipulation case comparing generated sequences. The argument being made is about the training objective: robot video world models are usually trained on reconstruction and perceptual similarity, which are only loosely related to whether the predicted sequence is physically coherent or the grasp is correct. Aligning the model to reward instead of pixels changes what it optimises for, and the grasps in the aligned sequence are the visible consequence.

Wu et al. · RoboAlign-R1 · Fig. 4 · arXiv:2605.03821

FLARE splits failures into two kinds and handles them differently. In-distribution errors are minor perturbations the policy could recover from, addressed by built-in retry robustness. Out-of-distribution errors are catastrophic states the policy has no path out of, addressed by reset skills bootstrapped from a multimodal LLM. The contrast on the right is with prior methods, which simply give up. Detecting a failure is only half the problem, and this is the other half.

Zhao et al. · FLARE · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2608.26645

Contact, and getting the world in

Vision-only prediction stops at the skin of the problem. These are the papers about touch, and about where the data comes from.

TacForeSight in two coupled stages. Stage 1 is a force-conditioned tactile world model: dual-finger tactile fields are encoded into compact latents, and tactile evolution is predicted conditioned on high-frequency wrist force and torque. Stage 2 uses those predicted tactile dynamics as contact priors for a lightweight flow-based policy. The asymmetry it exploits is that global force and torque change quickly and coarsely while local tactile fields change slowly and finely, so one is a good conditioning signal for the other.

Zang et al. · TacForeSight · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2606.11184

V-JEPA 2 pretrains on roughly a million hours of internet video plus a million images using a masked denoising objective in latent space rather than pixel space. The pretrained encoder then serves several downstream uses: aligned with an LLM for video question answering and action anticipation, or frozen and paired with an action-conditioned head trained on a comparatively small amount of robot data. Predicting in representation space rather than reconstructing pixels is the design choice that separates the JEPA line from the video-generation line.

Assran et al. · V-JEPA 2 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2506.09985

Closed-loop pick-and-place through V-JEPA 2-AC, driven by a sequence of goal images rather than a language instruction. The three goals are the object grasped, the object near the target location, and the object placed; highlighted frames mark where the model reaches one sub-goal and switches to the next. Planning runs at roughly 16 seconds per action, which is slow enough to be a real constraint and is exactly the cost the efficiency papers in section four are attacking.

Assran et al. · V-JEPA 2 · Fig. 10 · arXiv:2506.09985

The three sources of real robot data and what each one costs. Direct human collection gives high-quality samples but does not scale. Classical simulators generate enormous datasets but carry appearance and physics gaps to reality. Neural simulators trained on real data narrow those gaps but struggle with action-conditioned generation, so the video and the action drift apart. Every data strategy in this deck is a bet on one of these three, and this figure is the honest accounting of the trade.

Qin et al. · ComSim · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2604.11386

Video2Sim2Real starts from a single human manipulation video. Off-the-shelf estimators reconstruct a simulator-ready digital twin of the scene and extract robot and object motion priors; a keyframe refinement step fixes up the retargeted robot trajectory in simulation; then a decoupled sim-to-real policy is learned for reliable execution. The result is a demonstrated human skill transferred to a robot without any teleoperation, which is the cheapest cell in the previous figure's cost table.

Han et al. · Video2Sim2Real · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2606.08828

RoboSnap reconstructs a reusable, simulation-ready scene from one RGB photograph, with interactive physical assets rather than just geometry. The panels show what that unlocks: replaying recorded trajectories in the reconstruction, generating and augmenting task-specific data, and evaluating policies with meaningful sim-real correlation. The barrier this removes is scene construction, which has historically been the slow manual step in building any simulation benchmark.

Zhang et al. · RoboSnap · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2607.06699

A direct comparison of the two pretraining data sources. Egocentric human video offers roughly a million hours, low collection cost and open-world diversity, but only pseudo action labels recovered by retargeting human hands. Teleoperated robot data has precise action labels but roughly ten thousand public hours, high cost and limited scene diversity. Under a matched-scale comparison the human video wins on generalization, which is a provocative result given the field's investment in teleoperation rigs.

Ma et al. · HumanScale · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.20521

Deform360 is a multi-view visuotactile dataset covering 198 deformable objects, of which a subset is shown here. It is built to support 2D and 3D world models, contact detection and real-world robot planning together. Deformables are where vision-only prediction breaks down, because the same object under the same visible pose can behave completely differently depending on material properties the camera cannot see, and tactile data is the obvious missing channel.

Li et al. · Deform360 · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2607.05390

DeformMaster turns a phone-captured monocular video of a deformable object into an online interactive world model, recovering both the underlying physics and a high-fidelity appearance model. The panels show what that supports: long-horizon rollouts under actions never demonstrated, variation of material parameters via a Young's modulus slider, novel-view synthesis, and downstream robot use. The input being a single phone video rather than a capture rig is the part worth noting.

Li et al. · DeformMaster · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2605.09586

Four bimanual dexterous tool-use tasks, top to bottom: cutting bread with a chopping knife, cutting an apple with a fruit knife, cutting paper with scissors, and wiping a whiteboard. The columns are methods, and the comparison is deliberately unfair in the baselines' favour: ManipTrans and PhysGraph are given the ground-truth target from the demonstration, while DexFuture predicts its own target. Two hands coordinating indirect contact through a tool is among the hardest settings in this deck.

What is still missing

Two open problems: a prediction can look right and still break physics, and the real bottleneck may sit somewhere else entirely.

PhyGround decomposes a video model's physical reasoning into 13 separate physical laws instead of one holistic plausibility rating, so you can see which physics a model breaks rather than just that something looks off. The left panel shows the per-law breakdown, with gravity scoring 4.4 out of 5 while shadow scores 1.7, plus sub-questions asking why shadow failed. It is grounded in 459 annotators under quality control, and the resulting PhyJudge-9B judge model reproduces those human scores automatically with lower bias than a general VLM.

Lin et al. · PhyGround · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2605.10806

The same input prompts run through several state-of-the-art video models and through PhyWorld, in columns. The baselines break physical consistency in visible ways: colour shifts, backgrounds changing when they should not, artifacts appearing mid-sequence. These are not subtle scoring differences but things you can point at on screen, which is what makes this the most direct evidence in the deck that visual fidelity and physical correctness are separate axes.

Zhao et al. · PhyWorld · Fig. 2 · arXiv:2605.19242

Three rows: ground truth, the model's generation, and the two composited. The video model hallucinates the robot picking up a carrot that appears out of nowhere, and the calibrated uncertainty estimate lights up in magenta exactly where the invention happens. The significance is that the model is not merely wrong, it knows it is wrong at that location, which is the difference between a failure you can detect at runtime and one you cannot.

Mei et al. · Know When They Don't Know · Fig. 5 · arXiv:2512.05927

Four ways a generated video scores well while failing the task it was scored on. (a) Visual occlusion hides the interaction area and still gets 0.79 out of 1.0 on VideoAlign VQ. (b) Motion degradation, where the arm repeats trivial movements with no real interaction, gets 2.76 out of 5.0 on UnifiedReward alignment. (c) Background simplification scores 0.80 out of 1.0. (d) Physical invalidation, with the arm penetrating the object, still scores 3.0 out of 5.0 on a physics score. If reward is one of the missing interfaces, these are its blind spots.

Li et al. · Reward as an Agent · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2606.19990

Three intervention queries on the left: what happens if I release the ball, where should I release it to knock over the cup, would the cup tip if it were filled with honey. A visual world model answers all three wrongly, predicting the ball missing the cup and water exploding. The argument is that this failure is structural rather than a data problem, because distinct physical systems can look identical and only diverge once you intervene. Their alternative is to identify the simplest physical abstraction sufficient to answer the specific query being asked.

Thorpe et al. · Physically Viable World Models · Fig. 1 · arXiv:2605.30542

The concrete version of that claim. In (a) a fixed probing pour is used to infer an unknown liquid's viscosity from how it flows, fills, leaves residue and spills. In (b) that estimate then selects a pouring motion and stopping condition to transfer a target volume. The two rows are water and honey, visually near-identical in the source glass and completely different once poured. You cannot see viscosity, you can only probe for it, which is the cleanest illustration of why observation-predictive models are structurally incomplete.

Thorpe et al. · Physically Viable World Models · Fig. 5 · arXiv:2605.30542

Frames sampled at 0, 30 and 60 seconds from two scenes, comparing several long-video generation baselines against this method. The baselines fail in characteristic ways as the horizon grows: the subject disappears, structure distorts severely, or identity drifts into a different object. This is compounding error made visible, and it bounds how far any of the imagine-then-act methods in section four can actually roll out before their imagined future stops being about the same world.

Su et al. · Cycle-World · Fig. 3 · arXiv:2607.11836

The deck's closing argument, read left to right. Sources of physical experience feed grounding mechanisms, which produce robot-usable supervision, which feeds learning and control, which feeds deployment, with a self-learning loop closing underneath. The four missing interfaces sit in the middle columns: data, embodiment, world-model and reward. The callout inside reads built for actions, not pixels. Their claim is that the bottleneck was never policy scaling or the choice of backbone, but the machinery that turns the world's abundant unstructured behavioural data into something a robot can learn from.
